30 seconds · Opening. Imagine a young person chatting with someone new. A simple question comes in. They start typing a reply, and are about to share where they go to school. Halo offers a moment to pause before that message goes out. The young person can keep editing and choose what to share. Halo is an Android keyboard that gives supportive guidance on outgoing drafts. This is a fictional illustration of the moment we are designing for. Our sprint was about learning who we serve and how that guidance should work.

WEEK 5 / DEFINING

We cared about every risk.

And we wanted Halo to handle all of them.

Safer messaging Cyberbullying Personal information Image analysis Singaporean slang Offline AI Different languages Parent & child versions

We had a lot of ideas. A first version? Less clear.

35 seconds · Defining. At the beginning we were passionate about the many risks children face online. We wanted to cover personal-information sharing, cyberbullying, coercion and intimate-content risks. We also imagined image checks, local processing and Singapore-specific language support. Looking back, that enthusiasm made it hard to decide what the first MVP actually needed to do. Our first lesson was that caring about a problem does not automatically give us a focused product.

35 seconds · Stakeholder choice. Finding interviewees was harder than expected. We shared outreach messages and added a survey to make participation easier. We also had to decide whether Halo was aimed at parents or children. We chose parents as our initial research focus because they would likely be the paying customer. But the keyboard would be used by children. This distinction mattered: a parent's interest alone would not tell us whether a child would want the product.

RECESS WEEK / LISTENING

Concern came
with hesitation.

Parents worried about their children online.

Checking conversations could strain their relationship.

An app handling private messages had to earn their trust, too.

55 seconds · The turning point. During recess week we interviewed ten parents. Our notes describe a recurring tension. Parents worried even when nothing serious had happened, and wanted to know what their children were doing. At the same time, they knew that directly checking could offend their child or strain the relationship. Many relied on education and trust. When we introduced Halo, the app itself raised concerns about intrusion and security. We realised we were asking families to trust a new third party with something very sensitive. These interviews helped us see a possible role for Halo as a bridge between parents and children. This is what led us to reconsider our framing. These are themes from our sample, not a claim about every parent.

Source: w12 reflections.pdf, pages 1–2, Recess week.

45 seconds · Professor feedback. Two pieces of feedback helped us put the interviews in context. Professor Anand helped us see branding as more than colours or a logo. We needed a clear stance on the issue and a reason for parents to care about our approach. After our first class pitch, Professor Victor challenged fear-mongering as our primary strategy. Together, these conversations pushed us to reconsider both the purpose of Halo and the way we explained it. We took those lessons back into our team discussions.

40 seconds · Reframing. During Weeks 7 and 8, midterms gave us a pause in outreach and a chance to sit down together. We discussed the aim, user narrative and brand. 40 seconds · Including children. In Week 9 we interviewed five children. This brought us back to the people who would actually use the keyboard. But most were around fifteen or sixteen, at the older end of our intended nine-to-sixteen age range. We should be careful about treating their responses as representative of younger children. The clear learning is that children need a voice in shaping their experience, and our understanding is still incomplete.

60 seconds · Refining. In Week 10, Alan joined the team. We tried different models and paid close attention to wording, colour and the experience. This illustration makes that design question visible. The first version sounds like an official warning. The next explains the risk more clearly but still sounds formal. We then shorten the language and give the young person a choice. Finally, the softer blue and yellow, Halo logo and clearer action support the same tone. The point is that the AI's wording and the visual experience need to work together if we want children to listen.

LOOKING BACK / WHAT SURPRISED US

Some of the hardest work
was before the code.

01

Reaching people took more effort than we expected.

02

Our message needed as much thought as our features.

03

Each answer changed what we needed to ask next.

35 seconds · Surprises. Looking back, outreach was a substantial part of the work. Sharing a form widely did not automatically lead to many conversations. Then the conversations changed our questions. We began by thinking about online risks, but interviews raised relationship and security concerns. Professor and mentor feedback repeatedly brought us back to the narrative. We spent time on wording because a technically functioning prompt still needs to be something a child is willing to engage with. One thing we would change is to put a smaller first version and these stakeholder conversations earlier in the process.

Source: w12 reflections.pdf, pages 1–3. These are synthesized team reflections, not quotations or measured user outcomes.

40 seconds · Reflection through five lenses. The idea taught us to distinguish the parent customer from the child user. Execution taught us that AI behaviour and wording both need attention. As founders, we had to make choices about scope. As teammates, the realignment discussions reminded us to make our shared purpose explicit. As people, we recognised how easily enthusiasm could turn into trying to do too much. These are habits we want to carry into the next stage of the project.

55 seconds · Next steps. We plan a Google Play alpha, and we want to make it an opportunity to learn rather than treat distribution as the finish line. We want to understand whether the keyboard stays enabled and how useful or disruptive the prompts feel. Our child interviews need to include younger perspectives through an appropriate research process. We want to establish an Instagram presence to increase awareness and explain Halo to more families. We also want to visit social service agencies to hear a different perspective from practitioners who work with children and families. We want to ask what we might be missing, what concerns they would have about Halo, and what evidence or changes would make it more trustworthy. These visits could also help us understand whether collaboration is appropriate. These are future plans and proposed research questions, not completed interviews, partnerships or measured results.
