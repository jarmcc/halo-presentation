# ACT 1 — JARED
## The problem and the people · 0:00–3:15

### 0:00 · Slide 1 — A moment before sending (30s)

*The opening conversation loops automatically. Let it play; do not click until the end of the slide.*

**Jared:**

Imagine a young person chatting with someone new. A simple question comes in: "which sch u from?" They start typing — and they are about to share where they go to school.

Halo gives them a moment to pause. It is an Android keyboard that offers supportive guidance on outgoing drafts. The young person stays in control: they can keep editing, or choose what to share.

This is a fictional illustration. Our sprint was about learning who we serve — and how that guidance should work.

### 0:30 · Slide 2 — Our sprint (10s)

**Jared:**

This is the story of how our thinking evolved: the stakeholders we chose, how we reconsidered Halo's purpose, how we narrowed our scope, and how those decisions became our first prototype.

### 0:40 · Slide 3 — Defining (35s)

*Advance through the fragments as the ambitions appear.*

**Jared:**

In Week 5, we were passionate about the many risks children face online. We wanted Halo to cover personal information, cyberbullying, coercion, intimate content. We imagined image checks, offline AI, Singaporean slang, different languages — even separate parent and child versions.

Looking back, that enthusiasm made it hard to decide what the first MVP actually needed to do.

Our first lesson: caring about a problem does not automatically give you a focused product.

### 1:15 · Slide 4 — Our first website (30s)

**Jared:**

So we tried to make the idea visible. We built a website with a demo showing what Halo's prompts could look like. That made the idea concrete — and gave us something to share while we looked for parents to interview.

It was an important early thing we made, even before the app took shape. You can open it from the address on screen.

### 1:45 · Slide 5 — Choosing where to begin (35s)

*Reveal the children panel, then the lesson line.*

**Jared:**

Finding interviewees was harder than we expected. We shared outreach messages and added a survey to make participation easier. We also had to decide: was Halo aimed at parents or children?

We chose parents as our first research focus, because they would likely be the paying customer. But the keyboard would be used by children.

That distinction mattered. A parent's interest alone would not tell us whether a child would actually want the product.

### 2:20 · Slide 6 — Listening to parents (55s)

*Reveal the three points as you speak. Slow down here — this is the turning point.*

**Jared:**

During recess week, we interviewed ten parents. A recurring tension came up.

Parents worried even when nothing serious had happened. They wanted to know what their children were doing. But they also knew that directly checking could offend their child, or strain the relationship. Many relied on education and trust.

And when we introduced Halo, the app itself raised concerns about intrusion and security. We realised we were asking families to trust a new third party with something very sensitive.

These interviews helped us see a possible role for Halo as a bridge between parents and children. That is what led us to reconsider our framing.

These are themes from our sample — not a claim about every parent.

**Handoff:**

That tension is what changed our purpose. Adeeb will take you through that shift.

## Purpose and product · 3:15–6:50

### 3:15 · Slide 7 — Learning from our professors (45s)

*Reveal Professor Anand, then Professor Victor, then the bridge line.*

**Adeeb:**

Two pieces of feedback helped us put the interviews in context.

Professor Anand helped us see branding as more than colours or a logo. We needed a clear stance on the issue — and a reason for parents to care about our approach.

Then, after our first class pitch, Professor Victor challenged fear-mongering as our primary strategy.

Together, those conversations pushed us to reconsider both the purpose of Halo and the way we explained it. We took both lessons back into our team discussions.

Neither comment was about a feature. Both were about whether our message matched what we actually stood for.

### 4:00 · Slide 8 — Reframing our purpose (40s)

**Adeeb:**

During Weeks 7 and 8, midterms gave us a pause in outreach — and a chance to sit down together. We discussed the aim, the user narrative and the brand. We asked what Halo was actually for — not just what it could do.

We landed on a clearer purpose: to help children make safer choices, while respecting their relationship with their parents.

That was a change in how we framed the product. It was not proof that we had already solved the trust problem. But it gave us a test for every decision that followed.

### 5:10 · Slide 10 — Including children (40s)

**Adeeb:**

In Week 9, we interviewed five children. That brought us back to the people who would actually use the keyboard.

But most were around fifteen or sixteen — the older end of our intended nine-to-sixteen range. So we should be careful about treating their responses as representative of younger children.

The clear learning is that children need a voice in shaping their experience — and our understanding is still incomplete.

A parent choosing Halo is not the same as a child accepting it. Both have to say yes, for different reasons.

# ACT 3 — ZI ERN
## Progress and what comes next · 6:50–10:00

### 6:50 · Slide 12 — Where we are now (40s)

*Reveal the "Planned next" panel and the note.*

**Zi Ern:**

By Week 11, we had a working Android prototype, and we could give some parents and children a first look. We had also contacted seven social service agencies and were waiting on interviews.

The Google Play alpha is the next milestone. We plan to release it soon, and use it to learn how families actually use Halo — what happens outside a demo.

We are proud of getting from an idea to something real — but honest about the defects and the limited validation. A working prototype does not yet tell us whether families will keep using it, or pay for it.

### 7:30 · Slide 13 — What surprised us (35s)

*Reveal the three lines one at a time.*

**Zi Ern:**

Looking back, some of the hardest work happened before the code.

Outreach was a substantial part of the work. Sharing a form widely did not automatically lead to many conversations.

Then the conversations changed our questions. We started with online risks; the interviews raised relationship and security concerns. Professor and mentor feedback kept bringing us back to the narrative.

We spent time on wording because a technically functioning prompt still has to be something a child is willing to engage with.

### 8:05 · Slide 14 — What we learned (40s)

*Reveal the five lenses as you name them.*

**Zi Ern:**

The sprint changed how we think, in five ways. Each lens gave us a different way to judge what we had built.

The idea: parents and children bring different needs.

Execution: reliable checks need carefully worded guidance.

As founders: choosing a scope is part of building.

As teammates: a shared purpose needs explicit conversations.

And as people: our enthusiasm sometimes ran ahead of our focus.

None of these came from a single meeting. They came from the interviews, the feedback, and the choices we had to make together.

### 8:45 · Slide 15 — What comes next (55s)

*Reveal each next step as you introduce it.*

**Zi Ern:**

So what comes next?

First, a Google Play alpha — a chance to learn, not a finish line. We want to understand whether the keyboard stays enabled, and how useful or disruptive the prompts feel.

Second, younger perspectives in our child interviews. Our sample skewed older, and we do not want to generalise from it.

Third, an Instagram presence to explain Halo to more families, where parents already are.

Fourth, visits to social service agencies — to hear from practitioners who work with children and families. We want to ask what we might be missing, what concerns they would have, and what evidence would make Halo more trustworthy.

These are future plans and proposed questions — not completed interviews, partnerships or measured results. We would rather learn from a small, honest test than guess.

## Likely questions — short answers

**Does Halo read incoming messages?**
No. It checks the outgoing draft. The opening conversation is a fictional illustration.

**Is the app available now?**
There is a working Android prototype. A Google Play alpha is planned, not launched.

**Why research parents first if children use it?**
Parents are the likely payer, so their trust matters commercially. Children are the primary user, so their acceptance matters for the product. We need both, and our child sample still skews older.

**How does Halo handle privacy?**
The guidance only concerns the draft being written. Offline processing was an early ambition, not a shipped guarantee. The parent interviews showed that trust in a third party is a real barrier — we are not claiming it is solved.

**What is the biggest risk?**
That children find the prompts disruptive and disable the keyboard. Retention and willingness to pay are still unproven; the alpha is designed to test exactly that.

**Why not build all the features you listed in Week 5?**
Because a broad first version would have made it harder to learn what actually mattered. Narrowing scope was a deliberate decision, not a reduction in ambition.
